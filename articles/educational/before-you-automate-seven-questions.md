# Before You Automate: Seven Questions to Ask First

Automation is not the first step. Understanding the work is. If a process is unclear, inconsistent or full of exceptions, automating it can make mistakes happen faster and make them harder to spot.

Before choosing a chatbot, workflow tool, script or integration, ask these seven questions. They apply to ordinary rules-based automation as much as to AI.

## 1. Who owns the process?

Name one person who is accountable for the outcome, even if several teams perform the steps. The owner should understand the real process, approve changes and decide what happens when something goes wrong. If nobody owns the workflow today, automation will not create ownership for you.

## 2. What are the inputs — and are they reliable?

List where information comes from, who enters it, what format it uses and which fields are essential. Check for missing values, duplicates, inconsistent naming and outdated records. An automated process can only work with the information it receives. AI does not make unreliable source data reliable.

## 3. What are the exceptions?

Map the normal route, then list the cases that do not fit: incomplete orders, address mismatches, stock shortages, unusual approvals or system outages. Measure how often exceptions occur and decide who handles them. A workflow that handles 80 percent of cases but silently mishandles the other 20 percent may create more work than it removes. That percentage is illustrative, not a benchmark.

## 4. What is the baseline?

Record how the process performs now. Useful measures may include handling time, end-to-end cycle time, error rate, rework, backlog, missed deadlines and cost per transaction. Define the measurement period and how each metric is calculated. Without a baseline, you cannot tell whether a change improved the process.

## 5. What could go wrong — and how serious would it be?

Consider financial loss, customer impact, privacy, security, compliance obligations and operational disruption. Identify sensitive data, access permissions and actions that should never happen without a person checking them. Start with low-risk, reversible tasks. Keep high-impact decisions under appropriate human control.

## 6. What is the fallback?

Decide what happens if a connection fails, data is missing, an AI result is uncertain or the automation stops. There should be a visible error, a named person or queue for exceptions, a way to pause the workflow and a practical manual route. Never let a failed automation quietly look like a completed task.

## 7. What does success look like?

Choose a small number of measurable outcomes before building anything. For example: reduce median handling time without increasing error rates; shorten order processing while keeping manual review for address mismatches; or reduce repetitive copying while preserving an audit trail. Include total costs such as setup, subscriptions, maintenance, monitoring, staff training and exception handling.

## Then choose the simplest suitable solution

Some workflows need no automation — just a clearer procedure. Stable, predictable steps may suit ordinary rules or a simple integration. Repetitive work across systems may justify robotic process automation. AI can help where information is unstructured or language-based, but it introduces uncertainty and needs suitable controls.

Do not start with the technology. Start with the process, the evidence and the risk. Test one bounded workflow, keep a human fallback and compare the result with your baseline before expanding it.

**Practical next step:** choose one workflow that causes recurring friction and write down its owner, inputs, exceptions, baseline, risks, fallback and success measure. If you cannot answer those seven questions, you have found the work to do before automating.
